Sillage

You have ten thousand photographs and not one smell.

Smell reaches memory before it reaches language. One molecule of a stranger's perfume and you are in a hallway in 1998, involuntarily, completely.

Sillage is three things: a diary for smells, a training programme for your nose, and a place to keep what is on your shelf. All of it stays on this phone.

You cannot record a smell. You can encode one.

The trick is comparison. An adjective evaporates; a comparison sticks. Every entry asks you for one sentence of it, and hands you a shape to fill in.

This is the whole technique. It gets easier immediately.

A few minutes, most mornings.

Training is short and it uses what is already in your kitchen. Coffee, a lemon, cloves and mint will get you through the first week.

One notification, composed on this device. You can turn it off at any time.

How the scoring works

You shuffle the jars and you mark yourself. Sillage cannot smell anything, so the number on your accuracy curve is exactly as honest as you are. Marking a miss is worth more than marking a hit: it is the only way the app knows which shelf to send you back to.

What Sillage cannot do

  • Reach the internet. The app does not hold the permission, so it cannot open a connection at all.
  • Decode batch codes. That needs a brand database nobody can verify, so it is not here rather than half here.
  • Take photographs. There is no camera permission. Every bottle on your shelf is drawn from its own name.
  • Know the weather. Rain-triggered resurfacing needed a live forecast, so entries come back by date and by season instead.

Your notebook

An export is a plain file written to your Downloads folder by your own tap. It is not encrypted, so keep it where you would keep a diary.
